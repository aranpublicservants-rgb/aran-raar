Which recourse mechanism applies?

First identify the correct employer, employment status, union coverage, decision being challenged, and any applicable collective agreement. The same facts may raise different legal issues; choosing one process can affect another.

1. Employment grievances → FPSLREB adjudication

For matters within the Federal Public Sector Labour Relations Act: collective-agreement rights, certain discipline, termination, demotion, other eligible matters; initial grievance steps generally come first.

Decision and possible order: Grievance allowed, partly allowed, or dismissed; reinstatement, compensation, setting aside discipline or other authorized relief, depending on jurisdiction. Not every grievance can be referred to the Board.

Official grievance and adjudication rules

2. Internal appointments or lay-offs → FPSLREB staffing

For eligible complaints under the Public Service Employment Act: abuse of authority in merit or choice of process, certain language-of-assessment issues, lay-offs and revocations. External appointment investigations generally belong to the Public Service Commission.

Decision and possible order: Dismiss or uphold an abuse-of-authority complaint, revoke an appointment, direct an assessment or other authorized corrective measures. The Board cannot simply appoint the complainant to the job. A 15-calendar-day filing period commonly applies; verify the specific rule immediately.

Official staffing complaint guide

3. Human-rights discrimination → CHRC → CHRT

For federally regulated employment or services under the Canadian Human Rights Act. File with the Canadian Human Rights Commission (CHRC); only qualifying complaints referred to the independent Tribunal (CHRT) proceed to its adjudication. Other grievance avenues may need to be addressed.

Decision and possible order: Find discrimination established or dismiss complaint; cease discriminatory practice, correct barriers, restore lost opportunities, lost wages, pain and suffering (up to $20,000), and separate wilful/reckless compensation (up to $20,000), where the statutory conditions are met.

CHRC complaint information · CHRT decisions

4. Union representation or unfair labour practices

An FPSLREB complaint may challenge an employer's unfair labour practice or a bargaining agent's alleged breach of fair representation, subject to the law's tests. It does not automatically decide the underlying employer discrimination allegation.

Decision and possible order: Complaint upheld or dismissed; corrective orders under the applicable labour-relations statute.

Board labour-relations jurisdiction

5. Workplace harassment, violence or safety

The Canada Labour Code Part II and Work Place Harassment and Violence Prevention Regulations provide an employer-facing prevention and occurrence-resolution framework, distinct from a CHRA discrimination case or a collective-agreement grievance.

Decision and possible outcome: Notice of occurrence, negotiated resolution, conciliation or investigation, preventive recommendations and compliance follow-up. These mechanisms do not ordinarily adjudicate personal discrimination damages. Safety reprisals can have separate complaint routes.

Official federal harassment and violence prevention guidance

6. Whistleblowing reprisals or staffing investigations

Alleged reprisals for protected wrongdoing disclosures may be brought to the Public Sector Integrity Commissioner, with possible proceedings before the Public Servants Disclosure Protection Tribunal. External staffing, fraud and political-influence matters may fall under the Public Service Commission. These are distinct statutory pathways.

Decision and possible order: Investigation, referral or adjudication as authorized; the relevant statute defines the powers and possible corrective measures.

Integrity Commissioner reprisal complaints · Public Service Commission

7. Judicial review and appeals

A court examines a tribunal or public decision within the law's review or appeal framework. Not every challenge is a fresh hearing. The court may uphold, set aside or return a decision; separate processes govern permission and deadlines.

Decision and possible order: Dismiss review, set aside the earlier decision, remit it for redetermination or make other authorized orders. Always check the latest court result before relying on a tribunal summary.

Federal Court decisions · Federal Court of Appeal decisions

8. Informal support is not adjudication

An organizational informal-conflict-management service, an ombuds service where available, EAP or voluntary mediation can clarify concerns or resolve issues without a formal hearing. Availability varies by employer.

Important: These services generally cannot impose a binding tribunal judgment or pause filing limits automatically. Ask about confidentiality and verify formal deadlines separately.

How a decision-maker reaches a decision

This is a general orientation—not a single test applicable to every tribunal. Legal questions, evidence burdens and remedies differ by statute.

  1. Check jurisdiction and time limits

    Is it a grievance, staffing decision, human-rights complaint, safety occurrence or reprisal? Does the employee and employer fall under that law? File within the correct time limit.

  2. Identify the legal question

    Examples: adverse treatment connected to a protected ground; failure to accommodate; abuse of staffing authority; unjust discipline; unfair union representation. They are not interchangeable.

  3. Gather specific, relevant evidence

    Dates, notices, job criteria, assessment notes, comparative outcomes, emails, witnesses, medical functional limitations where needed, and relevant workforce or barrier patterns. Protect third-party privacy.

  4. Apply the correct evidentiary test

    Human-rights cases examine adverse treatment and its connection to a prohibited ground, including circumstantial or systemic evidence; employers may raise lawful justifications. Staffing cases assess statutory abuse of authority and other permitted grounds. Grievances examine the collective agreement and relevant statutes.

  5. Determine findings separately

    One case can find procedural unfairness but not discrimination; or find discrimination only on one protected ground; or dismiss a claim for jurisdiction without deciding the facts.

  6. Order only authorized remedies; check review

    The statute determines whether lost wages, reinstatement, appointment revocation, preventive action or CHRA damages are possible. A judicial review can uphold or set aside the outcome.

Deadlines require independent verification

Talking to your manager, union, ombuds or an informal conflict service does not automatically pause grievance, staffing, CHRC, reprisal or judicial-review filing deadlines. Not every employee is under the same employer or legislation.
